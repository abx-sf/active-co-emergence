Francis Bacon and Exposed Perception

Part of the research sequence — Activation

“I don’t think I have talent. I think I am simply receptive.”

The term perception comes from the Latin percipere: to grasp, to take through. It does not indicate only sensory data, but an operation of selection. What we see does not appear neutrally: it is filtered through bodily limits, attention, memory, habit. Every perception therefore involves reduction. Some information is retained, other parts excluded.

In ordinary experience, seeing and understanding almost overlap. A form is quickly recognized, associated with stable meaning, and integrated into what is already known. This process reduces uncertainty and makes the world readable. Habitable.

The work of Francis Bacon intervenes precisely at this point. It does not eliminate recognition and does not make the body unrecognizable. Instead, it interrupts the speed with which perception tends to close into a stable form.

“I have never tried to be horrifying.”

Perception never fully coincides with recognition. Before a figure is definitively identified, there is an unstable phase in which multiple possibilities remain open. Under ordinary conditions this interval lasts only briefly.

Attention also operates within this process. It does not simply register information: it concentrates the perceptual field, isolates elements, reduces dispersion. Through this selection, recognition becomes possible.

Interpretation does not arrive afterward either. It already occurs during the reading of the image. To interpret means to decide what something is. This decision allows orientation and continuity, but it always involves loss: other possibilities are excluded.

In Francis Bacon’s work this closure never fully completes itself. The figures remain recognizable as bodies, faces, or human presences, but certain elements prevent full stabilization. An anatomical axis breaks, a torsion diverts posture, multiple directions coexist within the same body.

“What I want to do is distort the thing far beyond appearance, but in the distortion itself bring it back to a recording of appearance.”

Deformation therefore does not function as an autonomous expressive effect. It intervenes in the relation between reading and recognition. The face remains readable as a face, but eyes, mouth, and direction of the gaze no longer coincide within a unified structure. The body maintains anatomical presence but loses stable continuity.

In this sense Francis Bacon does not work on pure perception, but on the moment in which interpretation attempts to stabilize itself without fully succeeding.

Photography takes on an important role precisely for this reason. The photographic images Bacon uses are not treated as models to reproduce faithfully. They are cut, isolated, manipulated, folded, worn down. They become operative material.

Cutting out a figure or insisting on a detail becomes almost a second exposure. No longer that of the camera, but that of the gaze that selects, isolates, and reactivates the image. Painting does not reproduce photography: it removes it from its initial evidence and reopens it perceptually.

“I think it is the slight removal from reality which returns me more violently to reality itself. Through the photographic image I wander within the image and extract from it what I believe to be its reality more than I can by simply looking at that reality. Photographs are not only points of reference; they often function as detonators of ideas.”

The use of Eadweard Muybridge’s photographic sequences, especially from Animal Locomotion, enters the same perceptual problem. Muybridge breaks movement into successive and observable phases. Bacon instead uses these fragmentations to compress multiple moments into the same body.

What begins as anatomical analysis gradually becomes deformed. Postures no longer follow one another linearly: they seem to overlap. A torsion may belong to one phase of movement while the direction of the gaze suggests another. Movement no longer organizes itself as a continuous sequence, but as the compression of different moments within the same body.

Movement therefore does not appear as fluid continuity. It appears as an accumulation of phases that resist forming a single stable sequence.

If torsion is reduced to a recognizable stylistic language, as part of contemporary art tends to do, the perceptual conflict structuring Francis Bacon’s work disappears. In Bacon recognition continues to operate, but never fully stabilizes.

“I have always hoped to put over things as directly and rawly as possible, and perhaps if something comes across directly people feel it as horrifying.”

The comparison with Pablo Picasso’s cubism remains useful precisely because it reveals a structural difference.

In cubism, fragmentation distributes the figure through multiple simultaneous points of view. The parts separate, but continue to construct a new spatial organization. Decomposition produces another equilibrium.

In Bacon something else occurs. The figure is not redistributed through space: it is compressed until stability begins to fail. Recognition continues to function, but encounters continuous obstacles. A face remains readable while losing anatomical continuity at the same time. A posture suggests movement without fixing itself into a single direction.

Cubism reorganizes vision. Bacon instead exposes the point at which vision loses stability.

For this reason his work cannot be reduced simply to emotional expression. It does not translate an internal state into image. It intervenes in the conditions that allow form to become readable.

In Francis Bacon’s works, perception, attention, and interpretation do not operate as clearly separated phases. They act simultaneously. Interpretation attempts to stabilize the image, but the form continues to resist complete resolution.

Recognition does not collapse, but stops fixing itself completely.

“Art is a method of concentration, of reinventing what we call fact, what we know of our existence.”

We continue to recognize the body, but can no longer fully stabilize it.
